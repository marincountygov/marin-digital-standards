# Principles

The foundation the rest of `content-design/` builds on.

## Content exists to serve a reader's task

Content should exist only when it serves a clear reader need with an accountable owner behind it. Before creating new content, confirm: there's a real user need, the audience is identifiable, similar content doesn't already meet the need, and the content will have a maintainable source of truth. If these aren't true, the answer is usually to improve existing content, not add more.

Organize around what the reader needs to do, not around internal department or program structure. A resident doesn't think in org-chart terms, and content that mirrors the org chart instead of the reader's task forces them to translate — that translation cost is the content's fault, not the reader's.

## Know the audience and their intent

Every piece of content has a primary audience (who it's mainly for) and may have secondary audiences (who else reads it, with different needs) and an internal audience (staff who use it operationally) — know which is which, and write for the primary audience first.

Frame what the reader is trying to do as one of a small set of intents:

- **Do** — complete a task or transaction (apply, pay, register, report).
- **Know** — understand a fact, rule, or status.
- **Find** — locate a place, person, document, or service.
- **Decide** — choose between options using comparable information.
- **Comply** — understand and meet a requirement.
- **Participate** — engage in a public process (comment, attend, vote, volunteer).

Content written without a clear intent in mind tends to explain everything the organization knows about a topic instead of answering the one question the reader actually has.

## One primary purpose per page

A page may support several related tasks, but it should have one primary purpose. Competing purposes or unrelated audiences on the same page are a sign it should split into two pages with clearer paths between them, not a sign the reader needs to work harder to find their part.

A useful test for whether a purpose is clear enough: can you state it as *"This page exists to help [audience] [complete a task or answer a question] so they can [outcome]"*? If the purpose only resolves to "provide information," it isn't specific enough yet.

## Plain language fits the audience, not the lowest reading level

Plain language means fitting the actual audience — it does not mean oversimplifying for an expert audience that needs precision, and it does not mean assuming residents can absorb bureaucratic or legal phrasing just because the topic is serious. See `plain-language.md` for the operating rules.

## Success is task completion, not traffic

Page views indicate demand, not success. Judge content by whether the reader completed their task, needed less support-line help afterward, or found what they needed on the first try — not by view count alone.

## Related standards

- `plain-language.md` — word choice, sentence structure, organization, testing.
- `voice-and-tone.md` — how Marin sounds.
- `inclusive-language.md` — respectful, accurate language for identity and sensitive topics.
- `terminology.md` — consistent terms and defined requirement words.
- `interface-writing.md` — headings, links, buttons, descriptions.
- `forms-and-errors.md` — writing (not structuring) forms and error messages.

Applying these standards to a specific piece of content — auditing, rewriting, scoring — is a `marin-skills` workflow (`plain-language-review`, `inclusive-language-review`), not something this document does.
