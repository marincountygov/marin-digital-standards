# Inclusive language

Respectful, accurate, non-stigmatizing language for content that refers to people, communities, identities, life circumstances, health, safety, services, eligibility, enforcement, or harm.

## Core principles

1. **Put people and their needs first.** Use language that helps people understand services, rights, responsibilities, risks, and next steps. Avoid defining people only by a condition, status, incident, diagnosis, income level, case type, or interaction with government.
2. **Be specific, accurate, and relevant.** Name a characteristic only when it's necessary for the content's purpose — don't add identity details or assumptions that aren't needed for the service, notice, or call to action.
3. **Prefer plain, direct, non-stigmatizing language.** Replace labels, euphemisms, bureaucratic phrasing, stereotypes, and blame-focused wording with active, concrete language that doesn't assign fault unless fault is legally established and relevant.
4. **Preserve self-identification and legally required language.** Don't override how a person, community, or group identifies itself. Keep statutory, regulatory, court, emergency, or program terms when required, but explain them plainly.
5. **Reduce harm in sensitive contexts.** Use trauma-informed framing for content involving violence, disasters, loss, enforcement, illness, disability, benefits, housing instability, abuse, or death. Avoid graphic, sensational, moralizing, or fear-amplifying language unless necessary for safety.
6. **Distinguish editorial guidance from specialist review.** This standard covers language choices, not legal, HR, civil rights, privacy, clinical, or law-enforcement determinations — those get escalated (see each category below), not decided here.

## Disability

Default to neutral constructions ("people with disabilities") when no preference is known; preserve identity-first language when a person or community uses it themselves ("Deaf community," "Autistic people"). Avoid ableist metaphors and labels (*crazy, insane, lame, blind to, wheelchair-bound, suffers from*) — use precise alternatives instead (*unreasonable, ineffective, unaware of, person who uses a wheelchair, person has*). Describe the barrier or support, not the person as the problem: *accessible entrance*, not *handicapped entrance*. State how to request an accommodation concretely — deadline, contact method, available formats — rather than a vague "reasonable accommodations are available." Include disability status only when needed for eligibility, service delivery, accommodation, or legal notice.

**Escalate** when content concerns ADA/FEHA, accommodations, discrimination complaints, service animals, disability benefits, medical verification, diagnosis, treatment, or protected health information.

## Gender, family, and identity

Use gender-neutral terms when gender isn't relevant (*they, person, resident, applicant, parent or guardian, spouse or partner*) rather than defaulting to *he/she* or gendered role terms. Use the name and pronouns a person provides; if unknown, rewrite around pronouns or use the person's name. Don't assume two-parent households, marital status, or gender roles — use *parent or guardian, caregiver, household member* where a specific relationship isn't required. When a program uses a legal category (*spouse, dependent, legal guardian, minor*), keep the required term and explain what it means in context.

**Escalate** when content concerns protected-class rights, harassment, discrimination, domestic violence, child custody, reproductive health, employment benefits, or legal parentage/identity documents.

## Trauma-informed language

Lead with safety and action: state what happened only to the extent needed, then what the reader can do, where to get help, and what happens next. Never imply a person caused harm by their choices, location, housing status, or reporting delay. Include graphic detail only when needed for identification, safety, legal notice, or health instructions. Prefer agency-preserving terms (*person who experienced harm, survivor, person seeking support*) over forced labels, unless legally required. For substance use and behavioral health: *person with a substance use disorder, person experiencing a behavioral health crisis* — never *addict, abuser, crazy, committed suicide* (use *person who died by suicide*).

**Escalate** when content concerns child or elder abuse, domestic violence, sexual assault, suicide, homicide, disasters, missing persons, behavioral health crisis, mandatory reporting, or active law-enforcement investigations.

## Age and income

Prefer *older adults* to *the elderly*, and describe children/youth/minors by actual age range and legal context. Never define people by poverty or benefits status — *people with low income, households with limited income*, not *the poor, welfare cases, needy families*. Use *people experiencing homelessness* or *people without stable housing*, never *the homeless* as a noun or *vagrants/transients*. State fees, penalties, waivers, and deadlines plainly rather than euphemistically hiding cost or consequence. Frame compliance content around the requirement, reason, deadline, and available help — not around implied irresponsibility.

**Escalate** when content concerns benefits eligibility, means testing, child welfare, elder abuse, homelessness response, debt collection, fines, evictions, or liens.

## Race, ethnicity, and national origin

Include racial, ethnic, national-origin, or immigration descriptors only when needed for context, services, data, eligibility, or civil rights reporting. Use the terms a community uses for itself — don't treat broad categories (*Hispanic, Latino, Latina, Latinx, Chicano*, or a country-of-origin term) as interchangeable without community context. Distinguish race, ethnicity, nationality, language, citizenship, and country of origin precisely; never use *foreign, alien,* or *illegal* as a noun for a person. Prefer *immigrants, people applying for asylum, people without current immigration status* over informal or dehumanizing terms; prefer *people who speak [language]* over *non-English speakers*. Use the specific tribe or nation name when known rather than collapsing distinct tribal nations into a generic term. Don't describe a community only through disparity, risk, or need — balance data with context and assets.

**Escalate** when content concerns civil rights, discrimination, immigration enforcement, demographic reporting, tribal consultation, or content that will be translated or targeted at a specific cultural community.

## Public safety and health

State the required action first in urgent content, with clear verbs (*Evacuate, Avoid, Call, Report, Boil, Stay away*) and who/where/by when/how. Don't define people by disease, exposure, vaccination status, or housing status unless relevant, and explain risk factors without moral judgment. Describe enforcement in terms of requirements, observed conditions, consequences, and appeal rights — never presume guilt before findings. Use *suspect, person of interest, person arrested, person cited,* or *person convicted* only when accurate to the actual stage of the process. Pair every hazard statement with next steps — protective action, contact, shelter information — not the hazard alone. Preserve official alert levels, disease names, incident names, and legal orders, explained in plain language.

**Escalate** when content concerns emergency orders, evacuation language, disease outbreaks, protected health information, criminal investigations, court matters, deaths, or hazardous materials.

## What this standard doesn't do

It doesn't make clinical, legal, HR, or civil-rights determinations, and it isn't a substitute for community review when content materially affects a specific community. Applying this standard to review or rewrite a specific piece of content — including the escalation call and the specific rewrite — is a `marin-skills/inclusive-language-review` workflow, not something this document does.
