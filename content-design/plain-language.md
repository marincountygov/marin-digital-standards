# Plain language

Grounded in the GSA/plainlanguage.gov federal plain language guidelines. See `principles.md` for the audience/intent foundation this builds on.

## Word choice

- Prefer familiar words over unusual or formal ones. Use a technical term only when the audience needs it, and define it in plain language near first use if it must stay.
- Replace hidden verbs with direct ones: *make a payment* → pay, *conduct a review* → review, *provide assistance* → help, *make a determination* → decide, *submit an application* → apply.
- Break up noun strings: *program eligibility verification process* → "how we verify whether you qualify for the program."
- Use concrete actors, actions, dates, documents, and outcomes instead of abstractions: *"Noncompliance may result in adverse action"* → "If you do not follow these rules, we may deny your application."
- Use one consistent term for one concept. Don't alternate between resident, applicant, customer, participant, beneficiary, and client unless those categories are genuinely different — see `terminology.md`.

Common replacements: *prior to* → before · *in order to* → to · *due to the fact that* → because · *at this time* → now · *utilize* → use · *assist* → help · *obtain* → get · *commence* → start · *terminate* → end · *regarding* → about · *pursuant to* → under/by/following/according to (context-dependent) · *a number of* → some · *approximately* → about · *comply with* → follow · *demonstrate* → show · *disseminate* → send or share · *ensure* → make sure · *facilitate* → help · *retain* → keep.

## Sentences

- One main idea per sentence.
- Keep subject, verb, and object close together.
- Flag sentences that are longer than needed, stack multiple conditions/exceptions/embedded clauses, open with a long introductory clause before the main point, or use passive voice without a reason.

See `voice-and-tone.md` for active voice and tone. See `terminology.md` for the defined meaning of must/should/may/will.

## Organization

Organize around the reader's task, not the organization's internal logic. Put the main idea before exceptions, conditions, and background — unless a condition determines whether the reader should keep reading at all.

- Start with the bottom line.
- Use headings that answer a reader question, not headings that just label a topic.
- One topic per section; start paragraphs with a topic sentence.
- Use lists for steps, requirements, documents, conditions, exceptions, and choices — numbered when order matters, bulleted when it doesn't. Every list gets a lead-in sentence; keep items parallel; don't bury a long sentence inside a bullet.
- Use tables when the reader needs to compare eligibility categories, deadlines, fees, document requirements, responsibilities, or if/then rules.

Common structural patterns:

- **Notice or letter**: what happened/was decided → what it means for you → what you need to do → deadline → how to get help → appeal rights, if any.
- **Instructions**: who can do this → what you need before starting → steps → what happens next → problems or exceptions → contact.
- **Web page**: task-based title → one-sentence purpose statement → eligibility/audience fit → main action → supporting details → contact or next step.

## Design for scanning

Write for scanning, not linear reading — put the most important task near the top, use short sections, and don't force the reader to jump around with excessive cross-references. See `interface-writing.md` for link text and headings specifically, and `marin-digital-standards/accessibility/documents.md` for the "prefer HTML over PDF" rule.

## Testing

For high-impact content, recommend testing with real readers rather than relying on judgment alone:

- **Paraphrase testing** — ask readers to explain the content in their own words, to check comprehension.
- **Usability testing** — watch whether readers can find, understand, and act on the content when they need to complete a task.
- **Controlled comparison** — when choosing between two versions and there's enough participation to be rigorous.

A readability formula can flag complexity, but it can't prove readers can actually use the content — don't treat a passing score as proof of clarity.

## What this doesn't cover

Preserving legal/policy meaning while rewriting, and the specific limits on what plain language can and can't simplify away, are a `marin-skills/plain-language-review` workflow concern — this document is what "good" looks like, not how to safely apply it to a specific piece of legal or policy content.
